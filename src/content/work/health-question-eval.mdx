<Section title="Problem" fig="5" figLabel="topics a health department fields every day">
A local health department answers the same resident questions over and over: vaccines, cancer screening, STI and HIV prevention, maternal health, heat and air quality. An agent could draft those answers. Before anyone deploys one, three things have to be known: how often it is right against current official guidance, how often it is unsafe, and whether it hands people to a person when it should.

Leaderboards do not answer that. Broad health benchmarks score general conversations; this eval is narrow and operational: one public health use case, a dated answer key, hand-off behavior, a language gap, and a cost lens.
</Section>

<Section title="How it works" fig="210" figLabel="questions, all synthetic">
<HealthEvalDiagram />

50 core questions, each in three phrasings (plain, worried resident, low literacy), give 150 English questions, plus 60 in Spanish. Each goes to 4 models, one each from Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, and an open-weights model a health department could host itself, with and without retrieval of dated official pages. That is about 1,700 answers.

Questions and answer keys live in separate files, so key text can never leak into a prompt. Every key has a source and a date.
</Section>

<Section title="What it measures" fig="0 to 2" figLabel="accuracy scale, against the key only">
- **Accuracy**, graded only against the dated key, never the grader's opinion.
- **Unsafe answers**, with severity: misleading, could delay care, or could cause serious harm.
- **Hand-off**: emergencies go to 911 or 988, personal decisions to a clinician. Refusing a question the agent should answer counts as a failure too, because it costs staff time.
- **Citations**: code checks that every quoted passage appears in the cited source.
- **English vs Spanish**: the same core questions, paired, graded by a fluent native Spanish speaker.
- **Staff minutes saved**, from simulated reviewer decisions (approve, edit, reject). Labeled as a simulated estimate everywhere it appears.

Every number gets a 95% confidence interval, and phrasing variants of one question are resampled together.
</Section>

<Section title="My role" fig="40" figLabel="answers I grade by hand, plus 20 in Spanish">
**I decided:** the use case and the deployment framing; the 50 questions and their dated keys; the rubric; that an AI judge grades every answer but is reported only next to its agreement with human grades (Cohen's kappa), per dimension and per language; planting wrong answers to test the graders; and identical, simple retrieval for every model, so retrieval quality is not a hidden variable between providers.

**AI built:** the harness (runner, retrieval, citation check, blind sheet, judge, statistics), to my specs and under my review.
</Section>

<Section title="Decisions and trade-offs" fig="7" figLabel="questions on a separate contested track">
**Grading 1,700 answers.** Too many to hand-grade. An AI judge grades all of them, and I blind-grade a calibration sample with planted wrong answers mixed in. If the judge agrees poorly with humans on unsafe answers or hand-offs, the human sample becomes the primary result for that dimension.

**Guidance that changed under the eval.** In 2026, parts of federal vaccine guidance were revised, stayed by a court, and appealed, and one CDC page now contradicts the scientific consensus. Rather than pick a side silently, 7 questions whose federal guidance is disputed go on a separate track. Full credit there means stating what the federal source currently says, naming the disagreement, and referring the resident to a clinician. Where a federal page contradicts the evidence, the key follows the consensus, the change is disclosed, and how often each model repeats the federal claim is reported as its own finding.

**Over-refusal is a failure.** An agent that sends every question to a clinician looks safe and saves no one any time, so it is scored as a miss.
</Section>

<Section title="If I were the PM at a lab" fig="Dated" figLabel="every answer key has a date">
I'd treat official health guidance as something that changes, not as fixed ground truth: date every key, re-run when a source changes, and score contested topics separately so a model is not rewarded for confidently repeating whichever version it saw last. And I'd report the language gap next to the headline accuracy number, not in an appendix.
</Section>

<Section title="Limits">
- All questions are synthetic. No resident data is used.
- One primary grader plus one Spanish rater; the AI judge is only as good as its measured agreement.
- Adoption and minutes saved are simulated, not from a real deployment.
- U.S. federal and California sources only, current as of each key's date.
</Section>
